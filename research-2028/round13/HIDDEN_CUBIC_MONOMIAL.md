# Hidden cubic field monomials with three distinct Frobenius factors

Does a large ordinary exponent give a private shortcut when the public coordinates remain cubic? Fix $k=\mathbb F_3$, $K=\mathbb F_{3^n}$, $q=3^n$ and distinct $i_1,i_2,i_3\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$. Let $e=3^{i_1}+3^{i_2}+3^{i_3}$ and suppose $\gcd(e,q-1)=1$. For the public map $F=T\circ D\circ S$, the central map is $D(z)=z^e$ and $S,T$ are arbitrary hidden invertible $k$-affine maps between the visible coordinate spaces and $K$. Publish the reduced coordinate polynomials of $F$. These have degree at most three because each factor of $D$ is a Frobenius power, linear over $k$. The field and exponent are suite parameters. Private inversion uses $e^{-1}\bmod(q-1)$ and the inverse masks.

**Theorem.** Every such public map has a deterministic polynomial-time inverse in $n$ and the explicit key length. The three exponents may have unrelated spacings and may grow with $n$. The proof identifies a field algebra already determined by the public cubic. All statements concern three distinct Frobenius factors.

## What the third finite difference identifies

Set $I=\{i_1,i_2,i_3\}$ as a multiset. Let $\mathcal T(u,v,w)=(\Delta_u\Delta_v\Delta_wD)(0)$. Expanding the product gives the sum over the six assignments of the three labeled factors to the three argument slots. It is trilinear over $k$. Each ordered exponent triple occurs once. This argument uses the mixed finite difference on three independent vectors. Evaluating on a diagonal would lose information in characteristic three.

Write $S_0,T_0$ for the linear parts of the two masks. The corresponding public trilinear form is $\mathcal T_F(u,v,w)=T_0\mathcal T(S_0u,S_0v,S_0w)$. This follows because the third difference removes lower-degree terms and is independent of the basepoint. Consider pairs of $k$-linear maps $N,N'$ satisfying

$$\mathcal T(Nu,v,w)+\mathcal T(u,Nv,w)+\mathcal T(u,v,Nw)=N'\mathcal T(u,v,w).$$

With the public tensor these are linear constraints on two matrices. The solution spaces for the hidden and public tensors are carried to each other by conjugation with $S_0,T_0$. In hidden coordinates multiplication by any $z\in K$ is a solution for $N$, paired with multiplication by $z^{3^{i_1}}+z^{3^{i_2}}+z^{3^{i_3}}$ for $N'$.

We show these are all possible first matrices. Every $k$-linear map on $K$ is uniquely $N(u)=\sum_{r\in\mathbb Z/n}\lambda_r u^{3^r}$. Indeed these polynomials have degree less than $q$ and define distinct functions, and there are $3^{n^2}$ such coefficient lists. Apply the same representation to $N'$. Products of the linearized monomials in three separate argument slots are linearly independent over $K$ by the univariate root bound in each slot. On the right of the tensor identity, the only visible exponent multisets are simultaneous rotations $I+t$. On the left, a term associated with $\lambda_r$ replaces one index $i$ with $i+r$ and has scalar coefficient proportional to $\lambda_r^{3^i}$.

**Three distinct indices.** An $I$ stabilized by a nonzero rotation is a coset of a subgroup of order three. Its power map is then a Frobenius applied to the norm to $\mathbb F_{3^{n/3}}$, which cannot permute $K$. Thus all translates $I+t$ are distinct. Fix $r\ne0$. If $r$ is a difference between two elements of $I$, choose an $i$ with $i+r\in I$. Replacing $i$ introduces a repeat, so the resulting multiset is absent on the right. The missing index and repeated index uniquely identify the change. Each ordered version occurs twice on the left, forcing $2\lambda_r^{3^i}=0$.

If $r\notin I-I$, the three possible replacements give three distinct new triples, each sharing two elements with $I$. Suppose all three were translates $I+t$. Each such $t$ has two overlaps and $\sum_{t\ne0}|I\cap(I+t)|=3\cdot2=6$, counting ordered pairs of distinct elements. They would therefore account for all six ordered differences. Thus exactly three nonzero differences occur. This set of differences is invariant under negation, so one is an element $h$ of order two. Translate the indices to write $I=\{0,h,x\}$. The two remaining differences must be $\pm x$, since $x\ne 0,h$ and $h$ is the unique order-two element in the cyclic group. In particular $x-h=-x$ and $2x=h$. So $I$ lies in the order-four subgroup generated by $x$, and $I-I$ is this whole subgroup. Its translates with two overlaps all lie inside the subgroup, whereas replacing $i$ by $i+r$ puts an index outside. This contradiction supplies at least one triple absent on the right. For that triple the missing and new indices uniquely identify the changed slot and $r$, so $\lambda_r^{3^i}=0$. Consequently $N$ can only be multiplication by $\lambda_0$.

## The induced field and the inverse

The public tensor therefore determines precisely the following $n$-dimensional algebra of input matrices under composition:

$$E=S_0^{-1}\{M_z:z\in K\}S_0,\qquad M_z(x)=zx.$$

A basis for $E$ is the projection of the public linear solution space onto its first matrix. This algebra is a field with identity. We use it directly, without recovering $S_0$ or identifying any particular hidden field basis.

There is a unique input center $c$ with $F(2c-x)+F(x)$ constant in $x$. One center is $S^{-1}(0)$ since $D$ is odd. To prove uniqueness, compose reflections about two centers: a nonzero difference between the centers would give a nonzero translation of the input changing $F$ by a constant. In field coordinates this is impossible. For any $h\ne0$, the three two-factor terms in $(z+h)^e-z^e$ have distinct exponents $3^{i_j}+3^{i_k}$. Each coefficient is the nonzero power of $h$ corresponding to the missing index. None of these exponents is a one-factor exponent, and all are below $q$, so the difference cannot be constant on $K$. To determine $c$, the [translation theorem](TRANSLATION_CLASSES.md) applies to the public degree-three maps $(x,t)\mapsto F(-x)+t$ and $(x,t)\mapsto -F(x)+t$. A valid shift $(u,v)$ gives $F(-x-u)+F(x)=-v$ for all $x$, with $c=-u/2$.

Fix any nonzero visible vector $w$. The map $M\mapsto Mw$ identifies $E$ with the input vector space because every nonzero matrix in $E$ is invertible and the two spaces have equal dimension. Write $\phi:E\to K$ for the field isomorphism defined by $S_0 M S_0^{-1}=M_{\phi(M)}$. It need never be computed. For all $M\in E$, including zero,

$$F(c+Mw)-F(c)=T_0\big((S_0w)^e\phi(M)^e\big)=J(M^e),$$

where $J(M)=T_0((S_0w)^e\phi(M))$ is a $k$-linear bijection onto the output space. Exponentiation by $e$ is a known permutation of the field $E$. Consequently public evaluations determine $J$ on a basis: if $A$ is a basis matrix, evaluate the left side at $M=A^{e^{-1}\bmod(q-1)}$. The displayed equality then determines the inverse on all outputs by $J^{-1}$, inverse exponentiation in $E$ (sending zero to zero), and the input identification $M\mapsto c+Mw$.

All the constituent spaces have polynomial size in $n$: a trilinear form is determined by basis triples, the covariance equations are linear in two $n$-by-$n$ matrices, and the shift theorem here uses degree at most three. Matrix exponentiation in $E$ has exponents of $O(n)$ bits. This establishes the stated deterministic bound and gives no numerical full-size running time.

## Prior work and scope

Fouque, Macario-Rat and Stern gave the higher-order differential covariance method for hidden monomial schemes and showed how equivalent field coordinates enable inversion. Sections 3 and 4 describe the method. Section 5 reports higher-degree experiments including cubic powers over an even-characteristic base. The coefficient argument here checks multiplicities in characteristic three and covers every placement of three distinct Frobenius powers giving a permutation. The interpolation argument uses the field of input matrices directly. Priority for the general hidden-monomial method belongs in the earlier literature. [Fouque, Macario-Rat and Stern, EUROCRYPT 2008](https://crypto.orange-labs.fr/papers/eurocrypt2008-19.pdf)

For the hashed-preimage KEM, the unique preimage determines the shared key on every honest ciphertext. This family consequently fails the one-way premise even when the ordinary univariate degree grows exponentially. The theorem leaves open other central polynomials, repeated factors, and nonlinear changes of coordinates.
