SG-DIFRA: An Attack-Driven NCSG KEM with Secret
           Incidence and MDS Coding

               Girish Nallan Chakravathy1 and Sreedevi Kannan2
              1 Tampere University, Tampere, Finland
              2 Birla Institute of Technology & Science, Hyderabad, India




   Abstract. SG-DIFRA is developed as an attack-driven research KEM built around
   nonlinear constraint-system geometry (NCSG) finite differences. The paper keeps
   four construction generations visible. The original Boolean complete-fiber design
   is broken by a characteristic-two period, public one-hot relations, repeated period-
   pair leakage, and end-to-end FO-message recovery routes. Candidate subsampling
   removes complete-fiber publication but creates a small-list consistency surface.
   Dense randomized labels remove the old parity identities but still leak a shared
   planted secret through public-fiber coordinate bias. Secret incidence then makes
   the public NCSG layer information-theoretically independent of the master secret,
   but its first binary reference profiles are themselves broken: at noise 10−3 a clean
   public information set occurs with constant probability, and the short hidden code
   creates searchable relations of weight roughly 5–11.
   The final construction, SG-DIFRA-MDS, retains the proved NCSG results and the
   secret-incidence separation but replaces the vulnerable binary ciphertext layer by
   a large-field hidden MDS layer. Labels and ciphertext symbols lie in K = F210 .
   The hidden selected labels form a generalized Reed–Solomon code under a secret
   full-rank linear embedding. Consequently the planted-label matroid has exact
   girth 𝑢 + 1, and every set of at most 𝑢 planted labels is linearly independent.
   Encryption adds exact K-symmetric symbol noise of rate 𝜈 = 3/16; bounded-
   distance Reed–Solomon decoding tolerates this noise in polynomial time. The
   previously successful clean-information-set strategy now requires expected random-
   basis work (1 − 𝜈) − 𝑁 , which is above 2191 , 2287 , and 2383 for the three reference
   profiles. The first possible all-planted linear relation has weights 65, 97, and 129,
   eliminating the previously identified low-weight relation attack by theorem rather
   than by sampling.
   We specify complete key generation, one-bit encryption/decoding, randomized
   message PKE, encapsulation, and implicit-rejection decapsulation. One-bit con-
   fidentiality reduces to two explicit average-case premises: MDS-HBCE, which
   hides the high-corruption planted MDS subset among NCSG buckets, and 𝑞-ary
   LPN over F210 with the stated symbol noise. The QROM KEM theorem then
   follows conditionally from the randomized-PKE bound, message-level correctness,
   finite-message search accounting, and the cited FO/MRE transform hypotheses.
   The Nallan Chakravathy–Sreedevi–Girish Differential Solvability problem
   (NCSG-DS) remains the NP-complete worst-case differential-solvability
   foundation; it is not misrepresented as a worst-case-to-average-case proof of the
   KEM assumptions. Three reproducible research profiles are provided together
   with attack-derived margins for the two newly identified attacks. They are
   experimental profiles, not standardized security levels.

   Keywords: post-quantum cryptography · key encapsulation · NCSG · Reed–
   Solomon codes · noisy linear equations · QROM · cryptanalysis

1   Introduction

A proposal becomes more credible when an attack is allowed to change the construction
rather than merely the wording of its proof. SG-DIFRA reached its present form through
that process four times.
    The first construction combined exactly regular finite-difference fibers, complete
one-hot publication, and a noisy binary parity ciphertext. Its intended reduction was
modular, but the sampled ciphertext distribution was not hard: characteristic two forces a
period in every directional derivative, and the public representation converts period pairs
into low-weight parity checks. Aggregating the full fiber produces a repetition channel
strong enough to recover the FO message or the complete noiseless image in concrete
synthetic regions. The public fibers also expose low-degree and MinRank structure.
    The first two repairs removed those exact mechanisms. Odd-characteristic maximum-
entropy derivatives remove the forced two-cycle. Dense randomized labels sever the
implication from algebra on assignment vectors to algebra on public ciphertext rows.
A second offensive pass nevertheless found coordinate bias because the public target
of every good constraint still depended on one shared ternary secret. Secret incidence
therefore reverses the generation order: the public NCSG predicate, target, and fiber are
generated first, and a private affine map later addresses an independently sampled point
of the already-public fiber. This yields an exact theorem: the entire public NCSG layer
contains zero information about the master secret.
    That theorem survived the next red-team pass, but the first SI reference profiles did not.
Their binary noise rate was 10−3 . A random invertible 𝑁-row public information set was
therefore error-free with probability (1 − 10−3 ) 𝑁 , equal to approximately 0.726, 0.639,
and 0.527 in the three profiles. Gaussian elimination recovered the ephemeral vector in
every executed bit-zero trial and separated bit zero from uniform bit one. Reuse of the
same public matrix across repetitions amplified the attack into complete FO-message
and shared-key recovery. Independently, the hidden binary code had dual distance only
slightly above four, giving practical meet-in-the-middle targets in the 226.5 –262.5 range.
    The final redesign addresses both failures together. The public-fiber independence
theorem is left unchanged. The binary short-code layer is replaced by a large-field
MDS layer over K = F210 . A generalized Reed–Solomon code supplies polynomial-
time bounded-distance decoding, exact minimum distance 𝑀 − 𝑢 + 1, and exact dual
distance 𝑢 + 1. This permits a substantial exact symbol-noise rate while making the first
all-planted linear dependency deliberately high weight. The final reference profiles use
𝜈 = 3/16, so the clean-basis attack becomes an exponential decoding strategy rather than
a constant-work distinguisher.

Contributions and proof status. The paper makes five kinds of claims. First, the strength-
ened NCSG-DS theorem proves NP-completeness with one vector-valued potential, one
global direction, zero target, and degree at most four. Second, the maximum-entropy
odd-characteristic derivative theorem and the secret-incidence independence theorem are

unconditional. Third, the MDS layer proves exact local linear-independence statements
through weight 𝑢 and gives polynomial-time decoding within half the MDS distance.
Fourth, the attacks that broke the previous finite profiles are re-evaluated at the final
parameters with explicit work factors and correctness tails. Fifth, end-to-end IND-CPA
and QROM IND-CCA security remain conditional reductions to the explicitly stated
MDS-HBCE and 𝑞-ary-LPN assumptions plus transform hypotheses. No claim equates
worst-case NP-completeness with average-case KEM security.
    The paper is deliberately a research construction. It does not claim standardization,
TLS suitability, universal superiority, or proof against every future classical or quantum
algorithm. Its stronger and falsifiable claim is that each confirmed structural attack that
caused a redesign has either lost a necessary algebraic premise, been closed by an exact
theorem, or been moved into a named computational assumption with a finite attack
budget.

The role of NCSG-DS. NCSG-DS is the mathematical substrate of the public local layer:
it supplies the differential-solvability language, the strengthened NP-complete worst-case
core, the universal table embedding, exact regular fibers, and the maximum-entropy finite-
difference ensemble. The final KEM theorem does not assert that NP-completeness alone
yields public-key security. The required average-case bridge is the NCSG-bucketed MDS-
HBCE distribution together with 𝑞-ary LPN. Thus the valid implication is conditional:
hardness of those sampled problems, correctness, and the QROM transform hypotheses
imply KEM security. A future worst-case-to-average-case reduction from NCSG-DS
would strengthen this chain but is not assumed.

3     Related work and design boundary
Hair and Sahai construct public-key encryption from two high-corruption CSP as-
sumptions and give restricted-model evidence for their sampled random-predicate
distribution [6]. Their work is the closest conceptual predecessor for the high-corruption
and label-extension viewpoint, but its hardness evidence does not transfer to the structured
SG-DIFRA distributions.
     Noisy parity remains a primary comparison point. Schmidhuber, O’Donnell, Kothari,
and Babbush obtain a nearly quartic quantum speedup for a planted noisy-inference
regime [12]; practical BKW techniques provide additional concrete baselines [14].
Błasiok, Lou, Rosen, and Sudan show that excellent expansion alone does not imply
noisy-𝑘-XOR hardness [13]. Accordingly, expansion and distance below are used only
for the exact statements they prove.
     The deployment hedge uses generic KEM-combiner theory. Giacon, Heuer, and
Poettering show that a parallel combiner with a split-key PRF core is IND-CCA secure if
at least one independent ingredient KEM is IND-CCA secure [16]. Millerjord, Stebila, and
Steckel extend the analysis to settings that expose post-processed ingredient interfaces [17].
The second ingredient in our optional hedge is ML-KEM-1024, standardized in NIST
FIPS 203 [18]. The hedge is not used as evidence for the experimental SG-DIFRA
assumption; it is a separate system-level protection against assumption failure.
     Reed–Solomon codes are used below for their exact MDS distances and efficient
bounded-distance decoding [1,2], not because exposed GRS structure is assumed
cryptographically hidden. Classical and recent work shows that public GRS-derived
codes can be structurally distinguishable and recoverable [3,4]. In SG-DIFRA the GRS
rows are hidden among independently random bucket labels and passed through an
arbitrary full-rank linear embedding; any algorithm exploiting that remaining hidden-
subspace structure is, by definition, an attack on MDS-HBCE.
     Finally, lower bounds in low-degree, SQ, SoS, or polynomial-calculus models remain
model-specific evidence. Recent counterexamples to broad low-degree conjectures
make it especially important not to convert such evidence into a statement about all
polynomial-time or all quantum algorithms [15].

6.7   Second red-team round: image recovery and public algebra

17    Limitations, falsifiability, and implementation boundary
The final redesign closes the two attacks that broke the previous finite profiles, but it does
not prove universal hardness. MDS-HBCE is new and must withstand robust subspace
recovery, generalized birthday search, code-equivalence ideas, algebraic statistics, and
quantum walks. 𝑞-ary LPN must withstand the best classical and quantum noisy-linear-
system attacks. Reed–Solomon’s MDS theorem proves the relation threshold and decoder
capability; it does not prove that a hidden MDS subset is pseudorandom. Recent attacks
on exposed GRS-derived cryptosystems reinforce this distinction [4].
    Likewise, NCSG-DS NP-completeness is not a cryptographic average-case reduction.
The final paper therefore uses the phrase NCSG-native KEM rather than claiming that
NP-completeness alone is the confidentiality engine. The open theoretical objective is a
reduction connecting an average-case NCSG distribution to MDS-HBCE or to a simpler
established assumption.
    A byte-level implementation must fix canonical encodings of F3 , F210 , supports, table
entries, labels, and ciphertext symbols; reject malformed or noncanonical representations
before decapsulation; domain-separate every random-oracle use; bind the complete
public-key encoding into deterministic coins; and compare re-encryptions in constant
time. Side-channel, fault, and randomness-failure resistance remain implementation
properties.


18    Conclusion

SG-DIFRA reached its final form by repeatedly discarding mechanisms that failed direct
attack. The Boolean construction failed because differential periodicity was published
through one-hot linear rows. Candidate subsampling removed complete-fiber exposure
but introduced a small-list surface. Randomized labels removed the old parity channel
but public targets still shared a planted secret. Secret incidence removed that correlation
exactly. The first SI profiles then failed for a different reason: low binary noise made
clean public information sets common, while the short hidden code created low-weight
public relations.
     The final SG-DIFRA-MDSconstruction retains every earlier closure and changes
the vulnerable layer rather than weakening those proofs. Public NCSG fibers remain
independent of the master secret. Hidden labels now use a large-field MDS code
whose first planted relation has exact weight 𝑢 + 1. Exact 3/16 symbol noise makes
the executed clean-basis strategy exponential at the reference dimensions while GRS
decoding preserves polynomial-time correctness. Complete PKE, encapsulation, and
decapsulation algorithms are specified, and the QROM theorem reduces a successful
KEM adversary to MDS-HBCE, 𝑞-ary LPN, correctness failure, finite-message search,
or the named transform terms.
     The resulting object is a research KEM, not a claim of unconditional quantum security.
Its strongest defensible contribution is a transparent construction-and-cryptanalysis chain
in which every confirmed break changes the mathematics of the next version and every
remaining computational premise is left exposed for the next cryptanalytic pass. The
finite-resource certificate of Definition 1 states what additional independent evidence

Every serialized object begins with a version identifier and a profile identifier. Field
