# A concrete, perfectly correct ternary public-key encryption scheme

This construction gives the receiver a specific secret to generate and use. Its ciphertext includes a finite difference of a cubic potential. Its security proof relies on **two specified parameter regimes of decisional random-code decoding** over the ternary field. The first hides receiver equations. The second hides the sender's payload. Neither experiment mentions ciphertext messages, KEM keys, chosen-ciphertext security, or private decryption. Quantum hardness in those regimes is an explicit computational assumption. For related definitions of decisional decoding using generator and parity-check matrices, see [Zémor, *Notes on Alekhnovich's cryptosystems*, pp. 1–2](https://www.math.u-bordeaux.fr/~gzemor/alekhnovich.pdf).

## Algorithms

Use column vectors over $K=\mathbb F_3$. Fix positive integers $m,t,k,w$, a nonnegative error weight $z$, and a public vector $\phi(x)$ of $a$ ternary polynomial functions of degree at most two. Write $d=k+a$, $L=m+t$ and $r=m-(t-1)w$. Require $tw\le m$. For the security experiments choose $d<r$ and $k<L$. Fix a public message encoder $C$ into $K^t$ with efficient decoding from any $z$ Hamming errors. Repeating every message trit $2z+1$ times is sufficient, with $t=\ell(2z+1)$ for $\ell$ message trits. The repetition implementation repeats each message bit as zero or one, and rejects a decoded symbol outside that alphabet. A second implementation injects the bit string into a [Reed–Solomon outer code](OUTER_CODE.md). The public layout specifies the order of these coordinates. A signed weight-$j$ error means a uniform $j$-subset of coordinates and independent uniform values in $\{1,2\}$ on its support.

1. **Key generation.** Draw an iid uniform $d\times m$ matrix $A$. Sample an ordered list of $t$ weight-$w$ signed vectors $s_j\in K^m$ with mutually disjoint supports, uniformly subject to that constraint. Set $h_j=A s_j$ and publish $M=[A\mid h_1\ldots h_t]$. The private key records the signed supports. A uniform permutation of the $m$ coordinates, divided into consecutive blocks of size $w$, samples the required supports.
2. **Encryption.** Draw independent uniform $u\in K^k$ and $x$ in the input space of $\phi$, and an independent signed weight-$z$ vector $E\in K^L$. Form $v=(u,\phi(x))$. Encrypt $b$ as
   $$Y=M^\top v+E+(0^m,C(b)).$$
   For $P(s,u,x)=s M^\top(u,\phi(x))$, the first term is precisely $P(s+1,u,x)-P(s,u,x)$, with total potential degree at most three. This directly specifies the public arithmetic operation.
3. **Decryption.** For each $j$, take $Y_{m+j}-s_j^\top Y_{1:m}$, numbering the $t$ final coordinates from one. Decode the resulting length-$t$ vector with the outer decoder. These secret checks are efficient even on ciphertexts for which public recovery is conjectured to be expensive. A KEM instantiation must also specify its validity checks and rejection behavior.

**Perfect correctness.** In the absence of errors, the $j$th residual is exactly $C(b)_j$, since $h_j=A s_j$. A nonzero error in a base coordinate affects at most one residual because the secret supports are disjoint. An error in a final coordinate affects only its corresponding residual. Thus at most $z$ residuals differ from $C(b)$ for *every* allowed set of encryption coins. The decoder recovers $b$ with probability one. The sender's auxiliary coins need not be reconstructed.

For example, choose $m=8,t=3,w=2,z=1$, a one-bit message repeated three times, and signed checks $s_1=e_1+e_2$, $s_2=e_3-e_4$, $s_3=-e_5+e_6$. Suppose a clean base vector is $(2,1,0,1,2,2,1,0)$. Its three clean sums are $(0,2,0)$. Message $1$ gives final coordinates $(1,0,1)$. Add $1$ to base coordinate three. The private checks now return $(1,0,1)$, whose majority is $1$. These small numbers illustrate correctness only.

## Two independent public experiments

Use raw distinguishing gap, the absolute difference of acceptance probabilities. Bounds below concern polynomial-time quantum computations on classical public keys and messages; any additional independent ideal oracle required by a KEM proof must be included in the stated resources when applying these assumptions.

* **Public-key experiment.** Given an iid uniform $d\times r$ matrix $B$, distinguish $(B,Bs)$ for a fresh signed weight-$w$ vector $s$ from $(B,U_d)$, where $U_d$ is uniform and independent. Bound the gap for each reduction by $\epsilon_1$. Only a single syndrome is supplied.
* **Payload experiment.** Given an iid uniform $k\times L$ matrix $G$, distinguish $(G,G^\top u+E)$ from $(G,U_L)$ with the independent distributions used during encryption. Bound the gap for each reduction by $\epsilon_2$. This generator-matrix formulation is decisional noisy random-codeword recognition.

For full-row-rank matrix distributions the second experiment follows from ordinary decisional syndrome decoding with an $(L-k)\times L$ full-rank parity-check matrix $H$ and a weight-$z$ error. Given $(H,y)$, draw a random ordered basis of $\ker H$ as the rows of $G$. Draw $W$ uniformly in the affine fiber $HW=y$. If $y=HE$, then $W$ is $E$ plus a fresh uniform codeword. If $y$ is independent uniform, $W$ is independent uniform. The basis is uniform among full-rank generator matrices by change-of-basis symmetry. For iid instead of full-rank generators put $\rho=1-\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-3^{i-L})<(1/2)3^{k-L}$. A raw full-rank distinguishing bound $\epsilon_{\rm SD}$ therefore implies $\epsilon_2\le (1-\rho)\epsilon_{\rm SD}+\rho$. The public-key experiment is already a parity-check syndrome experiment with its explicitly defined iid matrix law.

## IND-CPA against quantum computation

**Theorem.** For a classical-message public-key IND-CPA adversary with the resources covered above, the two-message raw gap is at most $\min(1,2t\epsilon_1+2\epsilon_2)$. Equivalently the excess success over one-half in guessing a fair challenge bit is at most $t\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2$. The challenger may receive its two messages after releasing the public key.

**Proof.** Replace the $h_j$ successively by independent uniform columns. When replacing one column, draw the other $t-1$ secret supports and their signs first, including dummy supports for already replaced columns. The available $r$ coordinates are their complement. Embed the $d\times r$ matrix of the first experiment in those columns of $A$ and fill the others with independent trits. Give the candidate syndrome the position of the selected $h_j$. Real side columns can be formed from their known secrets, and already replaced columns use independent trits. The real syndrome embeds a uniform signed support disjoint from all the others. The uniform syndrome embeds the next hybrid. Marginally $A$ is exactly iid in both cases. This proves the $t\epsilon_1$ bound, including all computations made after key publication in the guessing game.

After these hybrids, the entire public matrix is iid. Supply its first $k$ rows from the second experiment and sample the remaining $a$ rows $Q$ independently. Given the challenge messages, choose a fair challenge bit $\beta$ and fresh $x$. Add $Q^\top\phi(x)+(0^m,C(b_\beta))$ to the candidate length-$L$ vector from that experiment. In the noisy-word case this is exact encryption under the iid key. In the uniform case it remains uniform, independent of the challenge bit. Distinguishing the hybrids costs at most $\epsilon_2$. Public encryption lets the simulator answer ordinary encryption requests directly with fresh coins. The final guess of $\beta$ succeeds with probability one-half. Applying the triangle inequality and converting to the two-message raw-gap convention finishes the proof.

The independent uniform **linear** block is essential to this reduction. The nonlinear evaluation is genuine and the ciphertext has the stated ternary finite-difference representation, but the evidence for secrecy comes from the decoding assumptions. Substituting hardness of generic cubic equations, or removing the uniform linear block, would require a different proof. Polynomial hardness of the two stated distributions, exact classical or quantum work factors, priority for this construction, and practical compactness have not been established by this theorem.
